# Why China keeps coming back together, and Europe never did

## Two Ends of Eurasia

<!-- map: the wide eurasia plate opens; the eastern core lights, then the western core, at one scale, with a scale bar shared by both -->

Here is the eastern end of Eurasia. Here is the western end, at the same scale. Both are farming cores, close to the same size. One study, on its own definitions, puts each of them between two and a half and three million square kilometers. Now watch the states on them. In the east, the map keeps collapsing back into one color. In the west, it never does. Cores of a size, and completely different political histories.

Why?

## One Core, Several Cores

<!-- map: on the eurasia plate the camera settles on the east; the Yellow River and the Yangtze draw; the lowland mask between them lights with a "North China Plain" label -->

Start in the east. The Yellow River and the Yangtze come out of the highlands and cross the same lowland. Between them lies one continuous plain: flat, fertile, open from end to end. One reference study of China calls that plain the historic center of Chinese expansion.

<!-- map: the camera flies west across the plate; elevation masks light in turn on the Alps, the Pyrenees and the Carpathians; the peninsulas outline on "peninsulas" -->

Now the west. Europe's lowland does not come in one piece. The Alps lie across the middle of it. The Pyrenees close off Iberia. The Carpathians wrap around the Hungarian plain. And the western end breaks up into peninsulas: Iberia, Italy, Greece, Jutland, Scandinavia.

<!-- map: one lit core in the east against several separately lit cores in the west, held at one scale -->

One recent model of state formation starts from exactly this: terrain, and the productivity of land. It finds that Europe's mountains leave several distinct cores of roughly equal size, while China is dominated by a single vast plain between its two great rivers. Its authors are careful about what they have shown. Topography alone is enough to produce the pattern, they say, but it is not necessary. One productive core will do it on its own.

<!-- map: relief of both ends at one scale, high ground lit on both sides, so the eastern relief is visibly not the gentler of the two -->

And here the famous version of the argument gets into trouble. Jared Diamond argued that Europe's fractured land, its mountains and forests and indented coasts, kept large empires from forming. The standard reply comes from Philip Hoffman, and the economists who cite him put it bluntly: China is more mountainous than Europe. Its average elevation is higher. Real barriers divide its north from its south. And no natural barrier at all separates France from Germany.

<!-- map: the ruggedness on both sides dims away and only the cores stay lit: one in the east, several in the west -->

So rough ground is not the answer. The part of the geography argument that survives is the other part. One core worth owning, against several of equal size.

## China's Cycle

<!-- map: the china plate, the seven Warring States as separate fills; Qin's color spreads over all of them on "finished off" -->

In two hundred and twenty-one BC the state of Qin finished off the last of its rivals. Its king took a title nobody had held: First Emperor. Then he standardized the legal code, the bureaucracy, the writing and the coinage.

<!-- map: the Qin fill drops away to bare terrain, then the Han fill rises over the same ground with a Chang'an label -->

It did not hold. The First Emperor died, revolts broke out, and the dynasty was finished less than twenty years after its triumph. But what it had built came back almost at once. The Han ruled for four centuries, from Chang'an.

<!-- map: hold on the Han frame; the steppe polities north of the Han border light up above the farming limit -->

Hold on this frame. That is the Han state near its height. North of its border, the map shows steppe powers out on the grass. Keep them in mind.

<!-- map: the Han fill shatters into the 280 frame, then into the composed 560 map; a line runs from Luoyang south to Nanjing -->

Then the Han fell, in two hundred and twenty, and China came apart for most of four centuries. The Three Kingdoms first. Then a dynasty that reunified briefly. Then a court in flight: the capital left Luoyang for Nanjing.

<!-- map: the 560 frame stays up while a counter marks the regimes of the phase over empty sea -->

Those were not empty years. One count of this long division lists ten nomadic regimes and thirty-five settled ones, with their own capitals and wars.

<!-- map: the divided fills give way to a single Sui fill, then to the Tang frame; exam centers appear as dots on "examinations" -->

And then it came back. In five eighty-nine the Sui took the south, and the whole thing was a single state again. The Tang followed for nearly three centuries, and perfected the examinations that staffed the empire.

<!-- map: the Tang fill drops to the 980 frame, Song and Liao in two colors; then the 1140 frame, with the Song court south of the Yangtze -->

The Tang fell in nine oh seven. The Song put most of China proper back together, but never the north. Look at the map: other states hold the top of it. And then the Song court itself was pushed south of the Yangtze. Sources disagree about whether to call these centuries united at all.

<!-- map: the 1310 Yuan frame fills the whole plate in one color; the capital marker moves to Beijing -->

Then an outsider did the job. The Mongols took the Southern Song, and the Yuan became the first dynasty from beyond China to rule the whole of it. Kublai Khan moved his capital to Beijing, and took over the bureaucracy and the land tax he found there.

<!-- map: the Ming 1400 frame, then the Qing 1645 frame, then the Qing at 1800 reaching past the plate's edge -->

The Yuan fell, and the Ming rebuilt the same state. The Ming fell, and the Manchus rebuilt it again, keeping Ming institutions, including the Confucian civil service. Under the Qing the empire reached further than ever: Outer Mongolia, Central Asia as far as the Pamirs, a protectorate over Tibet, and Taiwan brought in for the first time.

<!-- map: the plate pulls back and the frames replay fast as a strip of united and divided spans -->

The list of dynasties is not the point. The return is the point.

## Europe After Rome

<!-- map: the europe plate, the 395 frame: the western and eastern halves of Rome in two colors over nearly the whole plate -->

Rome is the one empire that ever held most of Europe. This frame is three ninety-five: Rome's western and eastern halves. On one reading, Rome at that moment still ruled up to four fifths of the people of what we now call Europe. Not long after, empire in Europe was gone.

<!-- map: the 555 frame; Justinian's reconquest draws as a ribbon around the Mediterranean, with Constantinople labelled -->

Somebody did try. Justinian, ruling from Constantinople, took back North Africa and Italy. Look at the shape: a ribbon around a sea, not a block. It did not last. Within a lifetime his empire was fighting for its own survival, against Avars, Persians, and then Arabs.

<!-- map: the 800 frame; the Carolingian fill grows from the north, with a Rome marker on "crowned emperor" -->

The next attempt came from the north. Charlemagne's realm covered much of France, Germany, Austria, northern Italy and part of Spain. In the year eight hundred he was crowned emperor in Rome.

<!-- map: the 850 frame; the single fill splits into the three Frankish kingdoms, the middle one as a long strip -->

A generation later it was cut up. The Treaty of Verdun, in eight forty-three, split it into an eastern kingdom, a western kingdom, and a long strip between them. The family itself died out in nine eleven.

<!-- map: the 1000 frame; the Empire's territory lights in the middle of the plate and holds while the camera drifts along it -->

What held the middle of Europe afterwards kept the Roman name. The Holy Roman Empire ran from the coronation of Otto the First to eighteen oh six, when the last emperor gave up the title. For most of that time, the emperor could not behave like one.

<!-- map: the 1300 frame with the minor-states layer; seven elector markers appear on "seven princes" -->

He was elected, by seven princes fixed in law in thirteen fifty-six, and he was first among equals. Underneath him sat principalities, bishoprics, abbeys, counties and free cities, most running their own affairs.

<!-- map: the 1300 fill dims and a counter graphic builds over it, tile by tile, labelled "one account, the Empire's last decades" -->

On one account, about three hundred states still sat inside the Empire in its final decades. The counting is contested, and the smallest units are often left out.

<!-- map: the tiles collapse into a far smaller block of tiles on "left" -->

Then Napoleon went through it. On the same account, about forty of those states were left when he was finished.

<!-- map: the 1815 frame, central Europe; the confederation's members light inside a single outline -->

The Congress of Vienna gathered the survivors into a confederation of around forty states, in eighteen fifteen. Sources count its free cities differently. Either way, where an empire had been, there were dozens.

<!-- map: the camera pulls back to the whole plate and the 1914 frame fills in, every state a different color -->

Now skip a century. This is Europe in nineteen fourteen. Still a mosaic. After Rome, nobody ever put it back together.

## The Steppe and the Wall

<!-- map: back on the china plate; the Yellow River draws across the plain and the plain lights behind it -->

Before the frontier, one answer that does not work. The hydraulic thesis, the one associated with Karl Wittfogel: the great rivers needed flood control, flood control needed one large managing state, and so the rivers produced the empire.

<!-- map: the river line stays lit while the camera leaves it, so the plain fills the frame without it -->

The evidence runs the other way. Carneiro pointed out that in China, Mesopotamia and Mexico, full states appear well before large-scale irrigation. Others argue the bureaucratic state built the waterworks, not the reverse. At most, water reinforced something already there.

<!-- map: our own approximate wall route draws west to east, marked approximate on screen, with the steppe beyond it and the plain below it -->

Now the thing that does seem to matter. Look where the wall belt runs. It sits near the northern limit of land you can farm on rainfall alone. The wall marks the divide between the farmers and the herders of the steppe.

<!-- map: the farmland south of the route lights, then the grassland north of it, in turn -->

South of that line: farms, villages, grain. North of it: grass, and people who live by moving across it. The wall was begun as soon as Qin had unified the country, and four later dynasties rebuilt or extended it.

<!-- map: arrows advance from the steppe into the north, one after another, from different points along the route -->

That frontier never went quiet. One tally counts seven great waves of nomadic invasion since the first century. China was caught up in all but one of them. Western Europe, in two.

<!-- map: the arrows fade, and Chang'an and Beijing light up close under the wall route, with the safe south dark -->

Look where China's capitals sit. Chang'an and Beijing, both up against the threatened frontier, not safely in the south. By one count, those two cities were the capital for more than eight of every ten years that China spent united.

<!-- map: the wall route lights end to end while the camera runs along it, from the desert to the sea -->

Why would a frontier produce unity? The argument in this literature runs like this. One long frontier is cheaper for one large state than for ten small ones. Walls, garrisons and grain convoys on that scale only pay if somebody owns the whole line. So every time the country broke up, the north gave somebody a reason, and a means, to put it back.

<!-- map: a strip of decades builds along the bottom of the frame over the lit frontier, attacks above the line, united years below -->

It has been tested twice, on Chinese records. One study estimates that each extra nomadic attack per decade goes with a markedly higher long-run chance of unification, several percentage points on its own numbers, though its authors are careful about causation.

<!-- map: the same strip runs from the Qin frame to the Qing frame, the fills flickering between united and divided -->

The other works year by year, from Qin to the fall of the Qing, and finds the same direction. War with the steppe pushes towards unity. War between settled Chinese states entrenches division. Both call warfare the immediate cause, with ideology and institutions behind it.

<!-- map: cut to the europe plate; arrows advance from the east, the south, the north and the south-east in turn -->

Europe had no single frontier like that. The arrows come from every side: Huns, Avars, Magyars and Mongols from the east, Arabs from the south, Vikings from the north, Ottomans from the south-east. The reading here is that one frontier rewards one empire, while threats from four directions reward whoever is nearest.

## Why No European Hegemon

<!-- map: the 1000 frame fills, then dissolves into the 1300 frame on the same framing, so the new borders appear in place -->

The steppe story has a hole in it, and its own authors say so. After about eleven hundred, outside pressure on Europe eased. Europe went on splitting anyway.

<!-- map: the two frames sit side by side on one plate, three centuries apart -->

Watch these two frames, three centuries apart. More lines, not fewer. Something inside Europe was keeping it open.

<!-- map: papal lands light on the 1300 frame while the Empire's fill dims under them -->

One recent study puts the Church at the center of that. It argues that the medieval Church deliberately and effectively splintered political power in Europe, through alliances, proxy wars and crusades aimed at whichever ruler looked most dangerous. Which, often enough, meant the Holy Roman Emperor.

<!-- map: city dots light in order from southern England through the Rhineland to northern Italy, forming a corridor across the dimmed plate -->

Then add the cities. Charles Tilly described a corridor of city-states running from southern England through the Rhineland to northern Italy. On his reading, that corridor kept large empires from forming after about eleven hundred. That band through the middle of Europe belonged to nobody a conqueror could pick up cheaply.

<!-- map: the 1500 frame; France, the Habsburg lands, England and the Ottoman edge light one after another, none of them dominant -->

And look at the map in fifteen hundred. For any would-be emperor there is no weak side: several rivals of roughly equal weight hold it. On this reading, any one of them could be checked by the others.

<!-- map: back to the china plate: the core lights, then the wall route, then the single state fill returns over both -->

Put the two columns side by side. In the east: one core worth owning whole, one frontier worth holding, and a political idea that said the empire ought to be one. Standard writing from Qin, the Confucian classics, the examinations. None of those created unity. Each of them made the next restoration easier.

<!-- map: back to the europe plate: the several cores light, then the papal lands, then the city corridor, over the 1300 frame -->

In the west: several cores of equal weight, threats from every side, a Church working against the strongest ruler in sight, and a line of cities in the middle that nobody owned.

<!-- map: both plates at one scale again, each with its own lit elements held side by side -->

No single cause in either column. And the people who study this disagree about the weights.

## Two Objections

<!-- map: the strip of united and divided years returns, with the divided spans lit this time -->

Two honest objections to the story so far. The first: China was not always united. Count the years since Qin, and every tally here says more than half the time. Not all of it. The tallies disagree about how much more.

<!-- map: the divided frames come back in turn, 280, then 560, then 1140, each dated on screen -->

They disagree because they disagree about what counts as one authority, and about whether the Song centuries belong on the united side at all. Those divided eras were real: whole lifetimes, whole dynasties. To say that China stayed united is shorthand. What China did was come back.

<!-- map: the europe plate, 1500, with the city corridor relit; a split chart builds over the empty Atlantic -->

The second objection is bigger. A lot of historians argue that Europe's fragmentation was the best thing that ever happened to it. Walter Scheidel's version: escaping empire is the root of Europe's later rise, because it left competition and pluralism in place.

<!-- map: the chart keeps building while the camera drifts across the divided 1500 frame underneath it -->

That came with a bill. On one estimate, Europeans in the early modern period were at war with one another about one and a half times a year, and taxed several times harder than people in China. One study finds that unity bought China lower taxes and faster growth in peacetime, and more exposure when things went wrong.

## The Answer

<!-- map: the china plate recalls in order: the plain, the wall route, the state fill returning; no new cards -->

So, back to the two maps. On the eastern one: a single plain between two rivers, worth having whole. A frontier along the edge of the rain, with an enemy beyond it who never went away. Every collapse left the same prize and the same problem, and somebody always rebuilt the same answer.

<!-- map: the europe plate recalls in order: the cores behind the ranges, the invasion arrows, the papal lands, the city corridor -->

On the western one: cores of equal size behind the Alps, the Pyrenees and the Carpathians. Threats from four directions. A Church that broke the strongest ruler it could reach, and a band of cities nobody could buy. Every collapse there left several prizes, and several people who could reach one.

<!-- map: the eurasia plate again, both cores lit at one scale, the camera pulling slowly back -->

Not one cause, then. A core, a frontier, and then a habit. Europe never had the first two, so it never picked up the habit.

And one last thought. We usually ask this question as if unity were the normal state and Europe were the puzzle. From the other end of Eurasia, it looks the other way round.
