# Research: Why did China stay united while Europe never did?

Research pass of 2026-11-07. English. No script, facts ledger or storyboard was written. Page numbers below are the
journal's own pagination where I could read it (S01), otherwise section or PDF page.

**Repair pass, 2026-11-08:** the supplemental findings at the end supersede contradictory statements in the original claims, especially C02, C05, C12, C14, C25, C30 and C38. An attribution does not waive the two-independent-source requirement for spoken dates, figures or argumentative pillars. Optional unsupported statistics should be removed from narration, not rescued by saying “one study”.

## Central question

Why was Chinese imperial unity restored after every collapse, while no empire after Rome's ever held most of Europe for long? The evidence supports a combined answer, not one cause: China had one large productive core and one dominant external threat (the northern steppe), which together made reunification pay again and again, and a unity ideology and bureaucracy then made it self-reinforcing. Europe had several equal cores, threats from many directions, and, after about 1000, a Church and city-states that blocked a new hegemon. "China stayed united" is also an overstatement, since every tally here puts China at roughly 56-67% unified, not 100%. No single factor is settled, and the literature disagrees on weights.

## Contested points

**1. Geography: fractured land versus a single core (Diamond and the fractured-land literature).**
- For: Diamond (1997, p. 414), as quoted in S01 fn. 5 and S02, says mountains, forests and indented coasts left Europe with many cores. S02 models it and finds topography alone is sufficient but not necessary, with the productive North China Plain also yielding unification (S02 abstract).
- Against: Hoffman (2015) says China is more mountainous than Europe (S02 p. 2; S01 fn. 34; S03 fn. 6). Fairbank (1987), via S03 p. 5, says terrain "nurtured particularism and fragmentation" and that China's unity was "a triumph of human institutions over geography". Turchin (S16) says that if topography were decisive, unification would have come from the Yangtze or lower Yellow River, but the unifier came from the north 13 times out of 14 (his figure, not checked). Hui (2005, via S02 p. 2) calls Europe's fragmentation highly contingent.
- Weight: **open, leaning that geography matters only through the productive-core argument, not through raw ruggedness.** S02 itself says the broad (core) version carries the result. All the mountain-ruggedness evidence traces to Hoffman, whom I did not open.

**2. The steppe frontier (Ko, Koyama and Sng; Chen and Ma; Turchin).**
- For: S01 estimates that each extra nomadic attack per decade goes with a 6.3-8.3 point higher long-run chance of unity, and that China faced six of seven nomadic waves, Europe two. S03, with a separate annual dataset, finds agrarian-nomadic warfare strengthens unification. S16 argues the same mechanism ("warfare against the steppe horsemen") outweighs topography.
- Against or limits: S01 itself says its argument cannot explain why Europe's state count kept rising after 1100 (Figure 2), when outside threats had eased, nor why disunity periods in China shortened after 1300 (S01 p. 309). Its instrument-variable check "may not hold" (S01 p. 304). It works on Chinese records, which are written by the settled side.
- Weight: **the best-supported single mechanism for China's recurrence, weaker for Europe's persistence** (see 6).

**3. Hydraulic state (Wittfogel).**
- For: Needham and Huang (1974), cited in S01 p. 309 fn. 35, tie early unification to Yellow River flood coordination. S03 p. 12 describes water control as the base of the agrarian state "of what Wittfogel once termed Oriental Despotism".
- Against: Carneiro (S07 p. 4): archaeology suggests that in China, Mesopotamia and Mexico full states predate large-scale irrigation. Zhao, cited in S03 fn. 11: the bureaucratic-military state built the works, not the reverse. Wittfogel's thesis is heavily criticised.
- Weight: **against as a cause of unification**; at most a reinforcing factor. Carneiro addresses state origin, not unification of existing states, so the evidence is indirect.

**4. Writing system and unified elite culture.**
- For: Qin standardized writing (S09); S02 p. 5 calls the standardization of characters "a unifying force"; S03 argues an ideology of a single ruler was a second part of the explanation.
- Against: Fairbank, via S03 p. 8: the classics were composed before 221 BC, so the ideal of unity arose in disorder, before unity. S01 p. 309 calls scripts "random events". No quantitative test was found, and I found no opened source on Latin as a unifying European medium, which would be the obvious counterpoint.
- Weight: **open; likely a reinforcing, not an originating, factor.**

**5. Examinations and bureaucracy.**
- For: S03 pp. 7-8 links the exam to the Confucian-Legalist synthesis that held unity. S08: the exam lasted over 1,300 years and its abolition destabilised high-quota prefectures in 1911.
- Against: S01 p. 310: the exam "did not emerge overnight" and its full form is early modern; it came from long unification. S02 p. 4 treats it also as a brake on ideas.
- Weight: **it entrenched unity; it did not start it.** Open as to how much it mattered for the post-1300 shortening of disunity (S01 p. 309).

**6. The Church and competing sovereigns (Europe's side).**
- For: S05 argues the Church deliberately splintered Europe, lasting longer than bellicist theories predict. Hoffman and Tilly, via S01 p. 309, supply the Church and city-state arguments for Europe's fragmentation after 1100, which the steppe argument cannot cover.
- Against: S05 is a 2024 argument, not yet widely tested. S01 argues fragmentation first came from multi-directional threats before 1000, and that the Church could entrench itself only because no hegemon existed. The two stories may be complements.
- Weight: **open, but a gap in the pure steppe story is well documented**; the Church argument is a leading candidate for filling it.

**7. Fragmentation as a cause of the "European miracle".**
- For: Montesquieu, Jones (2003), Mokyr (2016), Scheidel (2019), as summarised in S01, S02 and S14. Scheidel: "nothing better could have happened to Europe" (S15).
- Against: S01 shows unity was cheaper in taxes and gave faster peacetime growth, but more shocks. S15 notes the "grisly irony" of prosperity rooted in constant war. S02 p. 5 says a centralized state could suppress ideas, but also that exams may suit a pre-industrial economy.
- Weight: **the majority view in this literature, but a consequence story, not an answer to "why unity?"** Treat it as the twist, worded as interpretation.

**8. How united was China, and how disunited was Europe?**
- Tallies differ: 1,418 of 2,131 years (S01, Wilkinson), 1,008 of 1,800 (S01, Ko and Sng), about 1,406 of 2,131 (derived from S03). Conquest dynasties count as unity in S03's scheme (it classifies by capital location, not ethnicity), and S09 counts the Yuan as ruling "all China". Whether the Song period, 960-1279, is "united" depends on whether Liao and Jin count as separate; S03 says fragmentation, S09 says reunified "most of China Proper".
- Europe: Rome held up to four-fifths of its people in 395 (S15), Charlemagne's realm was short-lived (S01, S10), and Napoleon and the HRE are also unifications of a kind. "Never united" is true of the continent after Rome, not of every part. Ko et al. also say the Roman Empire was itself a one-time event.
- Weight: **"more than half of the time" is safe; "always" is false.**

**9. Counting Europe's states.**
- Tilly's series (500 in 1500, 25 in 1900) is an estimate. The ending figure is quoted as 20, 25 and 30 in the literature I opened (S04, S05); Euratlas's strict sovereign-state count gives 114 for 1300 while the HRE alone may have held up to 1,000 sovereign units (S01 fn. 7). A "state" is defined differently in each.
- Weight: **open. Say "estimated", name the range, never state a single figure without its definition.**
