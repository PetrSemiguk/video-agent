# Why almost all of Egypt lives along the Nile

## The Strip

<!-- map: satellite plate, wide; the Egypt outline draws on "Egypt"; the Nile draws from Aswan to the Mediterranean on "the Nile", over the green strip the plate itself shows; a Delta label appears on "Delta" -->

This is Egypt, about one million square kilometers. Most of what you see is desert. Now look at this one thin green line. That is the Nile. Nearly all of Egypt's people live along it, or in the Delta at its end. Why here, and almost nowhere else?

<!-- map: camera pushes in along the river; a Cairo label appears on "Cairo", between the Delta and the valley; share graphic over empty desert with only "about 4% of the land" and "about 97% of the people", plus a small "sources differ" line -->

Most Egyptians live in the Delta, or in the narrow valley south of Cairo. Together, the valley and Delta are about four percent of Egypt's land. Recent estimates, including NASA's, put about ninety-seven percent of its people there. Sources differ on the exact shares, but a small part of the map holds almost everyone.

<!-- map: camera moves from the river out onto the desert; density graphic dated 1990s: about 1,500 per km2 anchored across the valley and the Delta with a "valley and Delta" caption; the desert anchor carries "a tiny fraction of that" and no number -->

In the nineteen nineties, density in the valley and Delta was around fifteen hundred people per square kilometer. In the rest of the country, it was a tiny fraction of that.

## The Water

<!-- map: camera drifts west across the desert with a "Desert" label on "desert", then flies to the coast with a "North coast" label on "north coast" (no rainfall shading exists); the Nile line brightens on "one river" -->

Move out over the desert, and the reason starts to show. By the FAO's estimate, almost no rain falls here. The north coast gets the most. So Egypt leans on one river. The FAO calls the Nile the main source of water for Egypt.

<!-- map: camera follows the Nile north from Aswan to the Delta (followLine) while the line redraws; a "Fertile mud (before the dam)" label sits on the plate's green strip; no flood animation and no valley polygon -->

That river once brought more than water. Its great flood usually came through Egypt and spread fertile mud over the fields beside it.

<!-- map: wider plate (about 24-42 E, 3-32 N); camera flies south past Egypt's southern border; the Ethiopian highlands light up on "highlands" as an approximate elevation mask from terrain tiles; an "around 86%" graphic with "common estimate" sits over empty ground -->

But follow the river south, far beyond Egypt's border. Here are the highlands of Ethiopia. By a common estimate, around eighty-six percent of the Nile's flow starts in Ethiopia. The exact share depends on which rivers are counted.

<!-- map: camera follows the rivers downstream; the Blue Nile draws first on "Blue Nile", the White Nile and the Atbara draw on their names, and the main stem carries on into Egypt on "another country" -->

The Blue Nile alone carries more than half of the water that reaches Egypt. The White Nile and the Atbara bring the rest. So most of Egypt's river starts in another country.

## The Dam

<!-- map: camera flies back to Aswan; a dam point with its label appears on "High Dam" (our own coordinate), dates 1960 and 1970 on a card; Lake Nasser lights up on "Lake Nasser" (Natural Earth lake) -->

Now come back to Aswan. Work on the High Dam began in nineteen sixty. It was completed in nineteen seventy. Behind it lies Lake Nasser.

<!-- map: camera moves from the lake north past the dam point; no dimming of the river line, the lake stays lit; a "Sediment retained (older study)" label appears on the lake on "sediment" -->

One account says the dam brought the Nile's devastating floods to an end. An older study says most of the river's sediment now stays in the lake. The FAO links the dam to less silt downstream and to more fertilizer.

<!-- map: camera runs from Aswan down to the Delta; the 97% graphic comes back as "NASA, published 2012"; no arrow or trend line -->

By our reasoning, if fertile mud were the whole answer, this strip should have lost its people. It did not. NASA's figure of roughly ninety-seven percent was published in twenty twelve, decades after the dam. Our reading is that water explains more of this map than mud does.

## The Answer

<!-- map: callbacks in the order of the sentences, no new card: valley and Delta labels, the Desert label, the Blue Nile and Ethiopian highlands, then the shares graphic -->

So why does almost all of Egypt live along the Nile? Our answer is that the valley and Delta are the one large part of this country with reliable water. The rest is mostly desert, with very little rain. The river brings its water from far to the south, mostly from Ethiopia. By recent estimates, about four percent of the land holds about ninety-seven percent of the people.

<!-- map: camera pulls back to the opening framing; the Egypt outline and the Nile line remain over the satellite plate -->

Look at that green line once more. Egypt's map of people follows water that mostly starts in another country.
